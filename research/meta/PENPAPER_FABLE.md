# Pen and paper: Fable's take (29 Oct 2026, late)

For Neo, via the orchestrator. Two pages. $0; nothing run. The live prior-art gate (research/audit/penpaper_gate) is the
orchestrator's; where I name prior work below I am naming what we have already read today, not auditing.

## 1. Honest take on the direction

Neo is right about the gap and right about the direction. Today's union adapter runs two procedures whose layout and
opcode cycle are fixed by the runtime; a third procedure means new rows and a new training run. That is one adapter per
operation with better packaging. The direction is right because every pen-and-paper procedure people actually use
(column arithmetic, long division, borrowing, Euclid's algorithm, polynomial division, Gaussian elimination, solving a
linear equation by transposition, fraction reduction) is the same object: a local rewrite rule on a 2-D grid of glyphs
with a cursor, a layout, and a stopping condition. We already have the executor for that object, certified, in the
browser, with language intact. What we do not have is generality across procedures, and the compile step from a
sentence to a layout. Those are two separate problems and they have two different answers; conflating them is how the
"adapter per operation" trap closes again.

Three things I would push back on. (i) Training from scratch: a from-scratch "little mathematician" has no language, so
it cannot take the text problem, and it throws away the one thing the pretrained VLM gives us for free, an eye that
reads glyphs exactly and a tongue that can write a program. From scratch belongs in the paper's architecture section
(an executor whose locality and addressing are built in, so certificates are once for all programs), not in this
week's demo. (ii) "Predict pixel frames like a video world model": chain-of-frames models do exactly that, globally
and uncertified, and they drift. Our frames are glyph grids; the pixels are the display. Keep it that way or the
certificate dies. (iii) "Fix mistakes and undo": with the canvas as the sole state there is no history to undo into.
What paper-and-pencil people really do is *check* (multiply back after dividing, substitute x back into the equation)
and overwrite. A check is itself a procedure for the same executor, and restoration (the model continues from whatever
is on the canvas) is already measured. So "undo" is a checking procedure plus overwrite, not a new mechanism.

## 2. The single idea: primitives × interpreter × compiler, with the plan drawn on the page

**Factor the skill.** Three parts, one adapter, one canvas:

- **Primitives** (certified once, tiny tables): digit add with carry (242 rows, have), digit multiply with carry
  (1,210, have), digit subtract with borrow (242, new), compare two digits (121, new), copy (12, new). Five tables,
  under 2,000 rows, certified once per device. Every pen-and-paper procedure above is a sequence of these.
- **An interpreter** (trained once, from synthetic random programs, never per procedure): the program is a short listing
  drawn in a strip at the top of the canvas (or in the cached prefix), one line per step: which primitive, which cells to
  read relative to the cursor, where to write, where to move, when to loop or stop. Each tick's fovea includes the
  current program line and the data cells; the model emits the glyphs and the next-line token. This is REGVM's result
  (Savant r3 ran 13 of 13 never-seen register programs bitwise exact, 11,882 ticks) moved to two dimensions. Certificate
  per program: its control rows, a few per line, computed on the device in seconds; the primitive rows are inherited.
  **New procedures need certification, not training.** That is the data-efficiency: the training set is random
  programs generated by code, and the thing learned is "read a line and do it".
- **A compiler** (the language half, uncertified, measured separately): sentence or photo → a layout and a program.
  "Solve 3x + 7 = 2026" becomes two lines on the page, "3x = 2026 − 7" (SUB) and "x = 2019 ÷ 3" (long division as a
  loop of SUB, CMP, copy), then the run, then x = 673 read back cell by cell. Training data is synthetic word-problem
  templates generated by code, no external corpus, and the held-out *templates* are the number that matters (P4 failed on
  comprehension, not notation; expect the same shape).

Long division is the right first target because its one non-local step, guessing the quotient digit, becomes local
by trial subtraction: subtract the divisor until the sign flips, count. Ten SUB ticks per digit at most, all certified,
about 10·n·m ticks for an n-digit dividend and m-digit divisor: a 6-by-2 division is about 120 ticks, half a minute at
the browser's tick rate, watchable. Linear equations with integer solutions are then two lines of program.

**Why the plan belongs on the canvas.** Drawing the program in a strip makes the page complete: the user sees what the
model intends and what it does, can edit either, and a screenshot restarts the run. It is also what makes the
certificate per program legible: the rows are "this line, this view, this write".

## 3. What is new, and what we must not claim

New, if the gate agrees: (a) procedures executed by the model's own forward passes with a per-program certificate
computed on the device, where Visual Sketchpad and Whiteboard-of-Thought execute in Python and MVoT appends image
tokens with no exactness; (b) zero-shot procedures: a held-out pen-and-paper algorithm runs exactly with no training,
only certification; (c) the canvas as the sole state at arbitrary length, with live edits followed, where
chain-of-frames video and text scratchpads keep a growing history (PENCIL erases in text, uncertified). Not new and not
to be claimed: next-frame prediction as an objective, local rules generalising in size (Neural GPU, NCA), erasure as an
idea (PENCIL), interpreters in LMs (Giannou, CAEF, our own REGVM), or anything called "reasoning". The honest headline
is "one certified pen-and-paper interpreter in a 0.8B VLM that still talks", and the honest risk is the compiler.

Today's inside-the-model facts fit this and are a figure, not a design input: the answer is written at layer 19, the
full-attention layer that re-reads the image tokens, and SUM's layer-16 class means lie on a number line in order 0 to
19. The visible canvas is where the state lives; the number line is how the eye hands it to the pen. Do not move the
canvas inside the model again; that is three collapses and one language-destroying positive in our record.

## 4. First tests, $0 to $20

1. **Dry-run long division and a linear equation with reference tables ($0, an hour).** Write SUB-with-borrow, CMP and
   COPY as reference tables, write long division and "ax + b = c" as program strips, and run them through the existing
   canvas runtime with the tables in place of the model. If the layout and control are right, the runtime is exact on
   1,000 random instances before any training exists. This is the table-driven dry run from SKETCHPAD2 and it costs
   nothing to be wrong here.
2. **Zero-shot control on the canvas (about $5, half a day).** Generate 300 random programs over the five primitives
   (random read offsets, writes, moves, loop counts); train one adapter on 250 of them with the program line in the
   fovea, recipe v2; hold out 50, including long division and the equation written as programs. Registered prediction:
   held-out programs exact on ≥ 45 of 50 after their control rows certify; kill at under 30 of 50, which would mean the
   canvas interpreter does not generalise and we are back to procedures as training sets.
3. **Compile baseline ($0, two hours).** 100 synthetic word problems from templates → canonical "ax + b = c" on the
   untouched 0.8B and 4B. Prediction: the 0.8B under 50% exact, the 4B over 80%; after SFT on 2,000 synthetic pairs
   (about $2) the in-template rate passes 95% and the held-out-template rate is the number we publish.
4. **Undo as check (free once 1 passes).** After a division, run "multiply back and compare" as a second program; corrupt
   one digit of the quotient by a user edit and confirm the check fails, the model overwrites, and the re-run is exact.

If 1 and 2 hold, the page shows a sentence turning into two drawn lines and a long division running itself, with the
certificate for that program computed in front of the viewer. If 2 fails, the fallback is the union adapter with long
division as a third trained program, which is what we have now, honestly labelled.

## 5. Beyond arithmetic: the factorisation that covers algebra (added 30 Oct, after Neo's push-back)

Neo's objection stands: five digit primitives give arithmetic procedures, not algebra, and more glyphs alone do not fix
it. The object that does is **term rewriting**, which is what computer algebra systems run underneath their step-by-step
mode. An expression is a tree of node records (id, symbol, child ids) held as a small table on the canvas. Every
pen-and-paper algebra step (distribute, collect like terms, transpose, factor, quadratic formula, differentiate) is a
local rewrite: match a small pattern at the top of a subtree, replace it, and *move* the pattern variables' subtrees as
ids without reading them. That keeps the view finite whatever the expression size.

Three layers: (1) **substrate**, certified once per device: the record heap plus field primitives (copy, compare,
allocate an id, digit arithmetic for coefficients and exponents); a 60-glyph alphabet grows these tables to a few
thousand rows, not the number of operations. (2) **rules**, certified per rule in seconds as control rows: each algebra
rule is a short program over the primitives; libraries for rationals, polynomial canonical forms, equation solving,
differentiation, Gaussian elimination. New mathematics is a new library, certified, not trained. (3) **strategy**,
uncertified, the LM's job: which rule, where, when to stop. Choose confluent rule sets (canonical forms; interaction nets
are the textbook case of local rewriting confluent by construction, every rule touching two nodes), so the planner can
change the time but never the answer. That is also the one place RL is safe for us: it buys speed, it cannot cost
exactness.

Training data at all three levels is generated by code: random substrate programs (interpreter generality), the rule
libraries with traces checked by a reference engine (SymPy as oracle, not as data), synthetic word problems for the
compiler. Geometry sits on the same layers: a fact table with deduction rules (as in AlphaGeometry's engine, with the LM
proposing constructions), coordinates by algebra, the figure as rendering. Unknowns: whether a 0.8B adapter learns a
pointer-following interpreter over a 60-glyph alphabet with language intact; step counts per algebra problem in the
browser; and prior art on neural term rewriting with certificates (search neural rewriting and interaction-combinator
runtimes such as HVM).

## 6. Breaking the wall: attention as the graph (added 30 Oct, after Neo's second push)

Neo: attention over tokens is a weighted graph; fine-tuned attention could write graphs and collapse nodes layer by
layer in a looped transformer, so the rewriting machine *is* the network. Precise form: nodes are tokens, pointers are
attention edges, one layer or loop iteration is one **parallel** rewrite step (every matched site rewrites, idle nodes
copy themselves, collapsed nodes become erased tokens), loop to the fixed point. Interaction nets are the formal object
(rules touch exactly two nodes; parallel reduction always valid; result independent of order); depth composing group
elements (our holonomy result, Liu et al.'s shortcuts) is the monoid special case.

Where the wall is: soft attention is a weighted graph, a certificate needs a hard local one (our no-finite-test theorem;
ANATOMY's 95%-not-100% pointer heads). So edges must be hard and local **by construction**: the pointer fields are the
attention mask, a layer becomes message passing over the net, the certificate is the rule table plus the copy rule, once
per device for all programs (programs are nets). Second wall: a forward pass cannot create tokens; pre-allocate blank
tokens that rewrites claim, with a trivial runtime rule appending blanks between loops. Consequence: this is a new small
architecture (looped, pointer-masked transformer trained as a graph reducer; tens of millions of parameters; synthetic
nets only; feasible in jax-js in the browser), grown not built, certified once; Qwen's own heads cannot be it without
losing the certificate. The LM stays compiler and voice; under confluent rule sets its strategy cannot change answers.
One set of weights for both is the funded from-scratch model.

Old: pointer networks, looped/universal transformers, Neural GPU, GATs, interaction nets, Neural Rewriter (RL), Giannou's
constructed computers. Candidate new: a trained pointer-masked reducer with a program-, size- and step-independent
finite certificate, planner-proof by confluence, driven by an LM that writes nets. Gate must search neural interaction
nets and transformer lambda-reduction. First test ($0–10, a day): interaction combinators (3 agent types, 6 rules);
train a small pointer-masked looped transformer on one parallel step over random nets; check exact normal forms on nets
10× larger/deeper and verify the 6 rules + copy rule through the trained layer in every port configuration; then encode
polynomial arithmetic as nets. Recommendation: the paper's architecture section and the build after the demo, not
instead of it; same theory as the canvas at a different depth.
